Migration routes do not just rewrite demographics; they change the chemistry of the ballot box. But exactly how? The question usually gets lost in the noise of political polling; we find the answer instead in those quiet, experimental periods when the state, not the migrants, decided where people would settle. Denmark's policy from 1986 to 1998 holds up a clear mirror. When refugees were placed in municipalities with little regard for local politics, studies found that in most municipalities, receiving more refugees raised support for anti-immigration parties. In the largest and most urban municipalities, however, the effect went the other way. A similar wind blew through Austria: where more immigrants lived, worries about jobs and about the quality of neighbourhoods channelled votes to the far-right Freedom Party, especially when the newcomers had little formal education. The core of the issue, however, lies in the depth of contact. Twice a year, in the dead of night, when we move our clocks an hour forward or back, we are not really playing with time but with an illusion of our own invention. Most governments now agree that this artificial pendulum swing has outlived its usefulness. But the real quarrel breaks out when the question is asked: "Which hour will we keep?" When the European Union put this rule, in place since 2001, to a public consultation in 2018, an overwhelming 84 percent of the 4.6 million respondents wanted the clock changes to stop. Although the European Parliament voted in 2019 to end the practice by 2021, the political chessboard among member states remains deadlocked. Imagine it: if one country chooses summer daylight and its neighbour opts for winter mornings, the two could end up an hour apart, and the map of Europe could turn into a patchwork of time zones. Russia acted as a laboratory for this dilemma. It moved the clocks forward in 2011 and left them there; but endless summer time did not last, and a law passed in July 2014 set the clocks back an hour for good, this time to permanent winter time. The bill for an earthquake isn't settled when the images of rubble fade from television screens; on the contrary, the real damage in the economy's capillaries begins that very day. The cost of collapsed bridges, pulverised factories and ruined ports – what we call "damage" – is merely the disaster's first face, counted only once. What remains invisible, stretching painfully over time, is the "loss of output": the value that city would have created over the years had it remained standing. Viewed from a distance, national economies are surprisingly resilient. Studies show that, on average, even very large disasters do not leave a lasting mark on national output, unless they are followed by radical political change such as a revolution. National averages, however, are masters at masking quiet, local tragedies. The January 1995 earthquake that struck Kobe, the pride of Japan, didn't just claim more than 6,400 lives; it wrecked much of what was then one of the world's busiest container ports. The World Health Organization counts about 830 million adults living with diabetes in 2022, up from around 200 million in 1990. Most have type 2 diabetes, which is closely linked to excess body weight and too little physical activity, and much of the rise has come in low- and middle-income countries. In much of the world, bread is the main way grain reaches the table, so the question of what bread does is a practical one. The research does not treat bread as a single food. It separates whole grain, in which the bran and the germ are kept, from refined grain, in which they are milled away and mostly starch remains. In a review of prospective studies, Aune and colleagues found that people who ate about three servings of whole grains a day had roughly a third lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes than people who ate almost none. Refined grains showed no such association. A later analysis of three American cohorts, which followed nearly 195,000 adults for decades, reached a similar result for whole-grain foods such as dark bread, oatmeal and whole-grain breakfast cereals. In 1974 the economist Richard Easterlin noticed a puzzle. Within a country, richer people said they were happier than poorer people. Yet as countries grew richer over the decades, average reported happiness barely moved. The puzzle took his name and shaped a long argument about whether growth makes lives better. With far larger surveys, Stevenson and Wolfers argued that the paradox mostly disappears. Life satisfaction rises with income both within and across countries, but it rises with the proportion of the change rather than the amount: doubling an income goes with a similar gain whether one starts poor or rich. Each additional dollar therefore buys less. The next question was whether the gains ever stop. Using about 450,000 survey responses from American residents, Kahneman and Deaton found that people's overall judgement of their lives kept rising with income, while their day-to-day emotions stopped improving at about 75,000 dollars a year. Later work by Killingsworth, sampling feelings in real time, found no such plateau. In 2023 the two sides analysed the data together. For most people, happiness keeps rising with income. How is sea level measured?

A rise of a few millimetres a year is built from tide gauges, satellites and a correction for land that moves.

Read more →

The sea is never still. Tides, waves, wind and air pressure move its surface by metres every day. Sea level is therefore not a single reading but an average, taken over long enough periods for those movements to cancel out. The oldest instrument is the tide gauge, which records the height of the water against a fixed mark on land. Some records are centuries old; Amsterdam's begins around 1700. Since 1933 the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level has collected such records, and it now holds data from more than two thousand gauges around the world. A tide gauge has a weakness: it measures the sea relative to the land it stands on, and land moves. Parts of Scandinavia are still rising as they recover from the weight of the ice sheets of the last ice age. Many coastal cities are sinking, often because groundwater is pumped from beneath them. Modern gauges are paired with satellite positioning receivers so that the movement of the land can be measured and separated from the movement of the sea. The Colorado River once ended in a wide delta on the Gulf of California. For most years since the 1960s it has not reached the sea at all. Its water is divided between seven American states and Mexico, and nearly all of it is taken for farms and cities before it gets there. The rules for that division were set in 1922, after a run of unusually wet years, and they promised more water than the river usually carries. China's Yellow River tells a similar story. In 1997 its lower course ran dry for 226 days, and the river failed to reach the sea for much of the year. Since 1999 the river has been managed under a central allocation that keeps a minimum flow at the mouth, which shows that a dry river is often a matter of rules rather than rainfall. The best-known case is the Aral Sea. From the 1960s the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, the two rivers that fed it, were diverted on a vast scale to irrigate cotton in Central Asia. The sea, once one of the largest lakes in the world, broke apart and shrank to a fraction of its former size. The common thread is withdrawal. The carbon books, reconciled

National inventories and satellite measurements are being read against each other – not as a headline, but as an accounting problem.

Every country that reports under the climate convention keeps a greenhouse-gas inventory: a ledger of what is burned, grown, stored, and released. Those ledgers are the official record. They are also slow, and they depend on methods that differ from one capital to the next.

Every country that reports under the climate convention keeps a greenhouse-gas inventory: a ledger of what is burned, grown, stored, and released. Those ledgers are the official record. They are also slow, and they depend on methods that differ from one capital to the next. A second set of books now comes from orbit. Instruments measure methane plumes and changes in atmospheric carbon dioxide, then scientists work backward toward the regions that could have produced them. The two records rarely match on the first reading. The work of recent years has been to ask why – a missing landfill, a revised emission factor, a wind field – rather than to declare a winner. Synthesis assessments still set the frame. Warming is unequivocally human-driven, and the remaining carbon budget is a quantity with a range, not a slogan. What the paired books change is the resolution: which basins, which sectors, which years deserve a closer audit.

    After ten million

    A megacity used to be an exception.

    Politics · 1 min read

    The United Nations counts a megacity at ten million inhabitants. The line used to mark a rarity. It now marks a class large enough to compare: how water, rail, and housing are planned when a city is no longer an exception in the tables. Population tables do not say how those residents are housed. Household surveys and national accounts still disagree on informal settlements, and the disagreement is the substance, not a footnote. A city can be officially growing while the count of serviced homes barely moves. The useful comparison is not a ranking of towers. It is whether the public record – census, utility connections, building permits – can be read in the same decade as the people it describes.

  2. 2

    How grain actually moves

    Wheat, maize, and rice cross borders as balance sheets before they cross them as ships.

    Economy · 1 min read

    Wheat, maize, and rice do not travel as headlines. They travel as contracts, port drafts, and seasonal windows. The public agencies that watch those flows publish balance sheets: production, trade, and stocks left at the end of the year, country by country. A balance sheet is not a forecast of price, and this atlas does not offer one. It is a way to see concentration. A small group of exporters still supplies a large share of the wheat that crosses a border. When one corridor slows, the sheet shows who else has grain to sell – and who imports enough that a delay becomes a domestic question. Reading the sheet well means noticing what it leaves out. Grain lost before the farm gate, cargo refused for quality, sacks that never meet a customs officer: none of these enter the line, and the gap is part of the record.

  3. 3

    The register of trials

    A study that never enters a public register is harder to find, and harder to count.

    Research · 1 min read

    A clinical trial is a plan before it is a result. The plan – who is enrolled, what is compared, which outcome counts – can be filed in a public register before the first participant is seen. The World Health Organization keeps a portal that searches the registries meeting its standard, rather than a single national list. The largest of those registries is run by the United States National Library of Medicine. It is one book, not the whole shelf. A trial recorded there may also be recorded in another registry, and a trial recorded nowhere is missing from both the count and the later reading of what was asked. Journals that follow the medical editors’ recommendations treat registration as part of the record, not as a courtesy. The point for this atlas is narrow. A result that cannot be walked back to a registered question is a thinner source than one that can.

The Milky Way is hidden from about a third of humanity. The glow is growing quickly. Between 2011 and 2022, tens of thousands of volunteers around the world reported which stars they could still see. Kyba and colleagues calculated from their reports that the sky was getting brighter by about 9.6 percent a year, which means it would double in brightness in less than eight years. Satellites miss much of this change, partly because they cannot see the blue light of modern white LEDs well. The body reads light as a signal of time. Special cells in the retina, most sensitive to blue light, report to the brain's internal clock, and light in the evening delays the release of melatonin, the hormone that prepares the body for sleep. White LEDs, now common in street lighting, emit a large share of their light in that blue range. Whether outdoor light is enough to change sleep is harder to show.
