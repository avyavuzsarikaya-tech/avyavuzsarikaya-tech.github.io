Who keeps the clock?

Most governments now agree the twice-yearly clock change has outlived its use.

Since 2001 the countries of the European Union have changed their clocks twice a year under one rule, Directive 2000/84/EC. In 2018 the European Commission asked the public what it thought. About 4.6 million people replied, and 84 percent wanted the changes to stop. The Commission proposed ending seasonal changes on 12 September 2018, and the European Parliament voted on 26 March 2019 to end them by 2021. The member states, meeting in the Council, have not settled their position. The difficulty is less the change than the choice that follows it: each country would have to decide whether to stay on summer or winter time, and neighbours could end up an hour apart. Russia tried both answers. Clocks went forward in March 2011 and were not set back that autumn, which made summer time permanent. Changes passed in July 2014 moved most of the country back an hour on 26 October 2014, this time to permanent standard time. In the United States the Senate passed the Sunshine Protection Act, which would make summer time permanent, on 15 March 2022. The House of Representatives did not take it up, and the bill lapsed. The question is usually argued from national polls, which cannot separate cause from mood. A cleaner test uses moments when the state, not the migrants, decided where people would live. Denmark did this between 1986 and 1998, placing refugees in municipalities with little regard to local politics. Comparing places that received more refugees with places that received fewer, Dustmann, Vasiljeva and Damm found that larger allocations raised the vote for anti-immigration parties in most municipalities. In the largest and most urban ones, the effect went the other way. Austria offers a second record. Halla, Wagner and Zweimüller found that communities with a larger share of immigrants gave more votes to the Freedom Party, the country's main party of the far right. The effect was tied to worries about jobs and about the quality of neighbourhoods, and it was stronger where the newcomers had little formal education. Contact changes the picture. How many years does an earthquake cost an economy?

For most countries, national output recovers within a few years.

Two numbers are often confused after a large earthquake. One is damage: the value of the buildings, roads, ports and machines that were destroyed. The other is output: what the economy produces in the years that follow. Damage is counted once. The loss of output is spread over time and is much harder to see. Across countries, the record is calmer than the pictures. Cavallo, Galiani, Noy and Pantano compared countries struck by very large disasters with synthetic versions of themselves, built from similar countries that were not struck. On average, even very large disasters did not leave a lasting mark on national output. The exceptions were extreme events that were followed by radical political change, such as a revolution. The national average can hide the local story. Kobe is the standard case. The Great Hanshin earthquake of January 1995 killed more than 6,400 people and wrecked much of the city's port, then one of the busiest container ports in the world. Japan's economy absorbed the shock. The World Health Organization counts about 830 million adults living with diabetes in 2022, up from around 200 million in 1990. Most have type 2 diabetes, which is closely linked to excess body weight and too little physical activity, and much of the rise has come in low- and middle-income countries. In much of the world, bread is the main way grain reaches the table, so the question of what bread does is a practical one. The research does not treat bread as a single food. It separates whole grain, in which the bran and the germ are kept, from refined grain, in which they are milled away and mostly starch remains. In a review of prospective studies, Aune and colleagues found that people who ate about three servings of whole grains a day had roughly a third lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes than people who ate almost none. Refined grains showed no such association. A later analysis of three American cohorts, which followed nearly 195,000 adults for decades, reached a similar result for whole-grain foods such as dark bread, oatmeal and whole-grain breakfast cereals. Does consuming more mean living better?

Income and life satisfaction rise together, but each step buys less than the one before.

In 1974 the economist Richard Easterlin noticed a puzzle. Within a country, richer people said they were happier than poorer people. Yet as countries grew richer over the decades, average reported happiness barely moved. The puzzle took his name and shaped a long argument about whether growth makes lives better. With far larger surveys, Stevenson and Wolfers argued that the paradox mostly disappears. Life satisfaction rises with income both within and across countries, but it rises with the proportion of the change rather than the amount: doubling an income goes with a similar gain whether one starts poor or rich. Each additional dollar therefore buys less. The next question was whether the gains ever stop. Using about 450,000 survey responses from American residents, Kahneman and Deaton found that people's overall judgement of their lives kept rising with income, while their day-to-day emotions stopped improving at about 75,000 dollars a year. Later work by Killingsworth, sampling feelings in real time, found no such plateau. In 2023 the two sides analysed the data together. For most people, happiness keeps rising with income. After ten million

A megacity used to be an exception.

The United Nations counts a megacity at ten million inhabitants. The line used to mark a rarity. It now marks a class large enough to compare: how water, rail, and housing are planned when a city is no longer an exception in the tables.

The United Nations counts a megacity at ten million inhabitants. The line used to mark a rarity. It now marks a class large enough to compare: how water, rail, and housing are planned when a city is no longer an exception in the tables. Population tables do not say how those residents are housed. Household surveys and national accounts still disagree on informal settlements, and the disagreement is the substance, not a footnote. A city can be officially growing while the count of serviced homes barely moves. The useful comparison is not a ranking of towers. It is whether the public record – census, utility connections, building permits – can be read in the same decade as the people it describes.

  1. 1

    How grain actually moves

    Wheat, maize, and rice cross borders as balance sheets before they cross them as ships.

    Economy · 1 min read

    Wheat, maize, and rice do not travel as headlines. They travel as contracts, port drafts, and seasonal windows. The public agencies that watch those flows publish balance sheets: production, trade, and stocks left at the end of the year, country by country. A balance sheet is not a forecast of price, and this atlas does not offer one. It is a way to see concentration. A small group of exporters still supplies a large share of the wheat that crosses a border. When one corridor slows, the sheet shows who else has grain to sell – and who imports enough that a delay becomes a domestic question. Reading the sheet well means noticing what it leaves out. Grain lost before the farm gate, cargo refused for quality, sacks that never meet a customs officer: none of these enter the line, and the gap is part of the record.

  2. 2

    The register of trials

    A study that never enters a public register is harder to find, and harder to count.

    Research · 1 min read

    A clinical trial is a plan before it is a result. The plan – who is enrolled, what is compared, which outcome counts – can be filed in a public register before the first participant is seen. The World Health Organization keeps a portal that searches the registries meeting its standard, rather than a single national list. The largest of those registries is run by the United States National Library of Medicine. It is one book, not the whole shelf. A trial recorded there may also be recorded in another registry, and a trial recorded nowhere is missing from both the count and the later reading of what was asked. Journals that follow the medical editors’ recommendations treat registration as part of the record, not as a courtesy. The point for this atlas is narrow. A result that cannot be walked back to a registered question is a thinner source than one that can.

    The carbon books, reconciled

    National inventories and satellite measurements are being read against each other – not as a headline, but as an accounting problem.

    Every country that reports under the climate convention keeps a greenhouse-gas inventory: a ledger of what is burned, grown, stored, and released. Those ledgers are the official record. They are also slow, and they depend on methods that differ from one capital to the next.

    Every country that reports under the climate convention keeps a greenhouse-gas inventory: a ledger of what is burned, grown, stored, and released. Those ledgers are the official record. They are also slow, and they depend on methods that differ from one capital to the next. A second set of books now comes from orbit. Instruments measure methane plumes and changes in atmospheric carbon dioxide, then scientists work backward toward the regions that could have produced them. The two records rarely match on the first reading. The work of recent years has been to ask why – a missing landfill, a revised emission factor, a wind field – rather than to declare a winner. Synthesis assessments still set the frame. Warming is unequivocally human-driven, and the remaining carbon budget is a quantity with a range, not a slogan. What the paired books change is the resolution: which basins, which sectors, which years deserve a closer audit.

    Most people still meet the state in a language. School guidance, a census note, a health instruction: each is a source. When they exist only in a language a city does not speak at home, the record is formally public and practically closed. UNESCO’s account of languages in education puts a measure on one part of that gap: a large share of learners are not taught in a language they understand well. Statistical systems have their own version of the same problem. Indicator notes, glossaries, and methods are often published once, in the working language of the office that produced them. Another office can cite the figure without being able to check the definition. The Sustainable Development Goals database is one of the few global ledgers that tries to keep the indicator list in more than one UN language – and even there, the depth of translation is uneven. The comparison worth making is simple. Take one claim. Count the languages in which a reader can open the underlying note, not a paraphrase. That count is the real width of the source.
