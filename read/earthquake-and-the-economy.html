How many years does an earthquake cost an economy?

For most countries, national output recovers within a few years. The places hit hardest can carry the loss for decades.

Kobe · Port-au-Prince · 7 October 2026

Type size
Type size

Two numbers are often confused after a large earthquake. One is damage: the value of the buildings, roads, ports and machines that were destroyed. The other is output: what the economy produces in the years that follow. Damage is counted once. The loss of output is spread over time and is much harder to see.

Across countries, the record is calmer than the pictures. Cavallo, Galiani, Noy and Pantano compared countries struck by very large disasters with synthetic versions of themselves, built from similar countries that were not struck. On average, even very large disasters did not leave a lasting mark on national output. The exceptions were extreme events that were followed by radical political change, such as a revolution. 1source 1

The national average can hide the local story. Kobe is the standard case. The Great Hanshin earthquake of January 1995 killed more than 6,400 people and wrecked much of the city's port, then one of the busiest container ports in the world. Japan's economy absorbed the shock. Kobe did not fully return to its earlier path: a study of the city two decades later found income per person still well below the level it would likely have reached, and the port lost traffic to rivals in the region that it never won back. 2source 2

Size relative to the economy matters most. When the 2010 earthquake struck Haiti, the official needs assessment put damage and losses at about 7.8 billion dollars, roughly 120 percent of the country's output in the previous year. 3source 3 A shock of that size cannot be absorbed by insurance, reserves or a quick rebuilding boom; it sets the starting point of the next decade.

What decides the answer, more than the magnitude of the earthquake, is set before it happens: how much of the economy sits in the area that shakes, whether buildings follow the code, whether firms and households are insured, and whether reconstruction money arrives quickly and reaches the people who lost most.
